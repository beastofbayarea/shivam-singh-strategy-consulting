# Stopping a Rewards Launch That Made Every New Customer Less Valuable

I led the strategy work for a rewards marketplace considering a direct consumer launch. I had identified that paid advertising would buy customers for more than their expected value, while employers could introduce the same service inside relationships people already trusted. I worked with Product, Finance, Partnerships, Operations, Analytics, enterprise clients, employees, and the executive team.

## I answered “how fast?” with “should we?”

During my Rakuten experience from June to December 2023, the marketplace had more than $50 million of launch momentum and planned to place 70% of spend into social acquisition. The base model showed lifetime value at only 0.8 times customer-acquisition cost.

I owned the stop-and-redeploy decision at executive level: challenge a funded consumer plan, make the downside legible across 10,000 scenarios, replace paid acquisition with enterprise distribution, and define the economics and concentration gates that would determine whether the new route earned more capital. The value was not a better campaign; it was preventing scale from multiplying a structurally negative customer relationship.

I ran 10,000 scenarios across media auction cost, conversion, churn, transaction frequency, gross margin, and lifetime value. I used a long-tailed distribution for media spikes, bounded churn probabilities, and a weighted range for customer value rather than one management forecast. In 68% of runs, the direct model lost more than $20 million in year one.

That was a modeled risk distribution, not an observed loss and not a claim that the business would fail with 68% certainty. It showed that plausible improvement in one input did not repair the structural unit-economics gap. I recommended stopping the launch.

## The replacement changed the route to the user

Rakuten's public description of its ecosystem explains the strategic fit: one membership and points system connects commerce, travel, financial, mobile, and partner services, aiming to lower acquisition cost and expand member value. The B2B2C design used employers and existing enterprise relationships to make that ecosystem available as an employee benefit.

The distribution contract included a minimum guarantee, an MSA-addendum route where an existing master agreement already existed, a standard onboarding playbook, activation and transaction reporting, renewal ownership, and support boundaries.

Two early routes reduced execution risk:

- an employee launch produced 14,500 active users in 30 days without waiting for an external master contract; and
- existing corporate-travel clients could use an addendum, reducing the deal cycle from a planning benchmark near nine months to 11 weeks.

No partner could contribute more than 15% of new users. That prevented a lower-cost model from replacing paid-media dependency with one-employer dependency.

## I designed scale evidence before the first logo

The analytics identified a useful behavioral signal: customers active on five separate days in a month were 60% more likely to retain. I used it to shape onboarding and partner selection, not to claim causation from a correlation.

Raw identities remained in regional systems. Analytics used deterministic hashed matches to connect exposure, activation, transaction, and retention. Early pilot effects were discounted by 30%–40% in the planning case because a first employer receives unusual attention.

The evaluation compared partner cohorts with a geographically matched direct-consumer control. Google's geo-experiment research supports random geographic assignment where practical; this project used matching rather than claiming full randomization, so residual selection bias remained. Scaling required the economics to repeat beyond the employee cohort and first external partner.

## Client results after the pivot

| Metric | Direct baseline/control | Scale gate | B2B2C result | Measurement |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Customer-acquisition cost | About $42 | Below modeled lifetime value | $8.50, about 80% lower | Attributable partner/onboarding cost per activated customer |
| Day-30 retention | 34% | Beat control after pilot discount | 67% | Day-0 activated cohort active on Day 30 |
| Monthly transactions | 1.8 per user | Improve repeat utility | 3.2 | Completed transactions per active customer-month |
| LTV:CAC | 0.8× | Above 1× with enough margin for model uncertainty | 4.5× | Discounted contribution value divided by acquisition cost |
| Payback | 18 months | Under 12 months | 4 months | Months for cumulative contribution to repay CAC |
| Contract cycle | Planning benchmark ~9 months | Use existing agreement where lawful | 11 weeks | First commercial step to executed addendum |
| Partner concentration | No portfolio guardrail | No partner above 15% of new users | 15 partners within 6 months | New-user share by partner and executed partner count |

These were operating-team outcomes from the B2B2C pivot. I owned the fact base, scenario model, stop recommendation, pilot design, measurement and concentration gates, and executive alignment. Product, Partnerships, and Operations owned contracts, launches, service, and realized P&L; I do not attribute every result to the strategy workstream alone.

## What made stopping a growth decision

The pivot worked because it changed acquisition, retention, contracting, privacy, and concentration together. Better ads could not have fixed a model that lost value on each additional user. A reversible partner route could.
